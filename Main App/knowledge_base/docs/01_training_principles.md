# Evidence-Based Resistance Training Principles

## Weekly training volume for hypertrophy
Weekly volume is counted as hard sets per muscle group per week. A meta-analysis by Schoenfeld, Ogborn & Krieger (2017, Journal of Sports Sciences) found a graded dose-response relationship: performing 10 or more weekly sets per muscle produced greater muscle growth than fewer than 5 sets. A practical target for most lifters building muscle is 10-20 hard sets per muscle group per week. Beginners grow well on the lower end (8-12 sets), while intermediate and advanced lifters often need 12-20 sets. Volume above roughly 20 sets per muscle per week shows diminishing returns and raises recovery cost.

## Training frequency per muscle group
Schoenfeld, Ogborn & Krieger (2016, Sports Medicine) concluded that training each muscle group at least twice per week produces better hypertrophy than once per week. When total weekly volume is equal, frequency matters less, but splitting volume over 2-3 sessions keeps per-session quality high (roughly 6-10 hard sets per muscle per session is a sensible cap). For that reason full body, upper/lower, and push/pull/legs rotations that hit each muscle twice per week are preferred over "bro splits" that hit each muscle once.

## Rep ranges and load
Muscle growth occurs across a wide range of loads (roughly 6-30 reps per set) as long as sets are taken close to failure (Schoenfeld et al., 2017, Journal of Strength and Conditioning Research). Maximal strength benefits more from heavy loads (1-6 reps, above 80% of 1RM). A practical approach:
- Compound lifts (squat, bench press, deadlift, rows, overhead press): 5-10 reps.
- Accessory and isolation exercises (curls, lateral raises, leg extensions): 8-15 reps.
- Bodyweight and band exercises at home: 10-25 reps, progressing to harder variations when 20+ reps become easy.

## Proximity to failure (RIR)
Reps in reserve (RIR) is the number of reps left before failure. For hypertrophy, most working sets should end at 0-3 RIR. Compound lifts are safer at 1-3 RIR; isolation exercises can be taken to 0-1 RIR. Beginners should stay at 2-3 RIR while they learn technique.

## Rest intervals between sets
Schoenfeld et al. (2016, Journal of Strength and Conditioning Research) showed 3-minute rests produced more strength and hypertrophy than 1-minute rests on multi-joint lifts. Recommended rest:
- Heavy compound lifts: 2-3 minutes.
- Moderate compound or machine work: 90-120 seconds.
- Isolation exercises and bodyweight circuits: 60-90 seconds.

## Progressive overload
Progress by adding reps within a target range, then adding load once the top of the range is reached on all sets (double progression). Example: 3 x 8-12; when all three sets reach 12 reps with good form, increase load by 2.5-5% and return to 8 reps. At home, progress by adding reps, slowing the eccentric (3-4 second lowering), adding pauses, or moving to a harder variation (e.g., incline push-up -> push-up -> deficit push-up -> archer push-up).

## Deloads and recovery
Plan a deload every 4-8 weeks or when performance stalls and fatigue accumulates: reduce sets by about 40-50% while keeping load similar for one week. Sleep of 7-9 hours per night supports recovery, strength and body composition. Avoid training the same muscle hard on consecutive days.

## Exercise order and session length
Place the most demanding compound lifts first in a session while fresh, followed by secondary compounds, then isolation work. Most effective sessions last 45-75 minutes and contain 4-7 exercises and roughly 12-22 total working sets.

## Warm-up
Do 5-10 minutes of light cardio or dynamic mobility, then 2-3 ramp-up sets of the first compound lift (e.g., 50% x 8, 70% x 5, 85% x 2) before working sets.
