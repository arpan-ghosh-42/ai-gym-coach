# Individual Factors: Sex, Experience, Body Size and Safety

## Training differences between men and women
The core principles of hypertrophy and strength training are the same for all sexes. A meta-analysis by Roberts, Nuckols & Krieger (2020, Journal of Strength and Conditioning Research) found men and women achieve similar relative muscle growth from resistance training, and women showed greater relative gains in upper-body strength. Women tend to be more resistant to fatigue during submaximal work (Hunter, 2014), so they often recover faster between sets and may tolerate slightly higher weekly volume and shorter rest intervals on accessory exercises. Many women prioritise glute and lower-body development; this is a preference rather than a physiological requirement, and upper-body training is equally valuable. Women should not avoid heavy loads - lifting heavy does not cause excessive bulk and supports bone density.

## Calorie and protein differences
Women generally have lower absolute calorie needs because of smaller average body size and lean mass (Mifflin-St Jeor uses -161 for women vs +5 for men). Protein targets per kg of body weight are the same (1.6-2.2 g/kg). During cutting phases women should avoid very aggressive deficits that may disrupt menstrual function; a 300-400 kcal deficit is a sensible default.

## Body mass index (BMI) and starting points
BMI = weight(kg) / height(m)^2. Under 18.5 is underweight, 18.5-24.9 normal, 25-29.9 overweight, 30+ obese. BMI does not distinguish muscle from fat, so muscular lifters often read as "overweight". If BMI is under 18.5, a cut is not advisable; prioritise a lean bulk. For people with BMI above 30 starting out, lower-impact exercise choices (e.g., box squats, step-ups instead of jumping, incline push-ups) protect joints, and a cut or recomposition is usually appropriate.

## Experience level
- Beginners progress fastest on simple programs with few exercises, 2-3 sets each, full body or upper/lower, and weekly load increases. Technique should be practised at 2-3 RIR.
- Intermediates need more volume, more exercise variety and planned progression (e.g., weekly rep or load increases over 4-6 week blocks followed by a deload).
- Advanced lifters benefit from periodisation, specialization blocks and careful fatigue management.

## Age considerations
Resistance training is beneficial at all adult ages. Older adults (50+) benefit from longer warm-ups, slightly higher rep ranges on joint-stressful exercises (8-15), and extra recovery time between sessions targeting the same muscles.

## Injury and safety guidance
Stop any exercise that causes sharp or joint pain (as opposed to muscular effort). Use controlled tempo, full but pain-free range of motion, and brace the core on compound lifts. People with existing injuries or medical conditions should consult a physician or physiotherapist before starting a new program and substitute exercises that do not aggravate the affected area (e.g., replace back squats with goblet squats or leg press for lower-back issues; replace overhead pressing with landmine or incline press for shoulder issues).
